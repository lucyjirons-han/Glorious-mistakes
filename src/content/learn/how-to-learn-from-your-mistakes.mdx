Everyone says you should “learn from your mistakes”. Hardly anyone tells you how. Usually the advice stops right there, leaving you to stew on it in the shower until something wise falls out. (It rarely does. Mostly you just get wrinkly.)

The good news is that learning from a mistake doesn’t need a journal with a lock, a retreat or a whiteboard. It needs two questions and about two minutes.

## The two questions

Every entry on Glorious Mistakes is built around the same pair:

1. **What happened?**
2. **What did I take from it?**

That’s it. They’re simple on purpose. The first gets the story out of your head and onto the page, where it’s easier to see clearly. The second turns the story into something you can actually use next time.

### Question one: what happened?

Write down the facts, like you’re telling a friend over a cuppa. What were you trying to do? What actually happened? What happened next?

A few tips:

- **Stick to what happened, not what it “says about you”.** “I forgot to attach the file” is useful. “I’m hopeless” isn’t. It’s also not true.
- **Include the bit just before it went wrong.** That’s usually where the lesson is hiding. Were you rushing? Tired? Juggling three things?
- **Feel free to make it funny.** Dramatic re-enactments are welcome. A bit of humour makes the whole thing easier to look at.

### Question two: what did I take from it?

This is where the magic happens. Look back at your story and ask: if I could whisper one tip to past me, what would it be?

Lessons come in all sizes:

- **Practical:** “Put the attachment in *before* writing the email.”
- **Big picture:** “I say yes to things when I’m tired. Maybe sleep on it.”
- **Gloriously silly:** “Eggs do not belong in the microwave. Ever.”

They all count. A small, specific lesson is often more useful than a grand one, because you’ll actually remember it.

## A worked example

Here’s a real-feeling example of the two questions in action.

**Headline:** Sent my CV to a job with another company’s name in the cover letter

**What happened?** I was applying for a few jobs in one night and reusing my cover letter. I changed the address at the top but forgot the line near the end that said how excited I was to join *a completely different company*. I only noticed when I reread it the next morning. I’d already hit send at 11:40pm.

**What did I take from it?** Two things. First: late-night applications are a trap, so I’ll write them in the evening and send them the next morning after one last read. Second: if I reuse a letter, I’ll search it for the old company name before I send it. (I emailed them a quick, cheerful correction too. They still offered me an interview.)

Notice what that entry does. It doesn’t beat anyone up. It finds the moment things went sideways (rushing late at night), then turns it into two concrete habits. That’s learning from a mistake in a nutshell.

## Make it a tiny habit

One reflection is handy. A regular habit is where it really pays off, because you start to spot patterns. Here’s how to keep it small enough to stick.

### Keep it short

Two minutes, tops. You’re not writing a memoir. A headline, a few sentences and a one-line lesson is plenty.

### Do it soon

Reflect while it’s fresh but after the initial cringe has worn off. Later that day is usually the sweet spot. If you’re still too wound up, our guide on [getting over an embarrassing mistake](/learn/how-to-get-over-an-embarrassing-mistake) can help first.

### Anchor it to something you already do

Habits stick better when they ride along with an existing routine. Try reflecting while the kettle boils, on the bus home or every Friday afternoon as a little “week in stuff-ups” review.

### Look back now and then

Every month or so, scroll through your old entries in [My mistakes](/mine). You’ll probably laugh, and you might notice a theme (hello, 11:40pm decisions). Spotting a pattern is where one-off lessons become real change.

### Celebrate the logging, not just the lesson

Some days the lesson is just “well, that happened”. That’s fine. Showing up and writing it down is the habit. The insights come with time.
