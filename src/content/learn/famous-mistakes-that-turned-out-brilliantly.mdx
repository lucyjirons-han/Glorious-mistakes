Most stuff-ups end with a slightly burnt dinner and a lesson about timers. But every so often, someone makes a mistake, notices something odd and asks, “Hang on, what’s that?” And the world gets a little bit better.

Here are six famous ones. We’ve stuck to well-documented stories, and where the popular version is more legend than fact, we’ll say so.

## 1. Penicillin: the mouldy dish

In 1928, Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming was growing bacteria in his London lab. One dish had been left on the bench instead of in the incubator, and a mould had grown on it. Around the mould, the bacteria had died.

Plenty of people would have binned it. Fleming got curious. The mould was *Penicillium*, and what it made became penicillin, the first antibiotic.

**The fine print:** Fleming’s notes are sketchy, so the exact date is debated. And he couldn’t turn penicillin into a medicine. That took Howard Florey (an Aussie, as it happens), Ernst Chain and their Oxford team, around 1940. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the 1945 Nobel Prize.

**The lesson:** A messy bench isn’t great. Noticing what’s happening on it is.

## 2. Post-it Notes: the glue that didn’t stick

In 1968, 3M chemist Spencer Silver was trying to make a super-strong glue. Instead he made a weak one that peeled off easily and could be stuck down again. Nobody knew what to do with it.

Silver kept telling colleagues about it anyway. One of them, Art Fry, was fed up with bookmarks falling out of his church hymn book. Around 1974 he realised Silver’s “failed” glue would make the perfect bookmark: sticky enough to stay put, gentle on the page.

After a few test markets, Post-it Notes launched across the US in 1980. Twelve years from “oops” to office essential.

**The lesson:** A mistake might be a solution still looking for its problem.

## 3. The microwave oven: the melted chocolate bar

In the 1940s, Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer was working on magnetrons, the devices that power radar. One day, standing near a running magnetron, he found the chocolate bar in his pocket had melted.

Rather than grumbling about his trousers, he experimented. Popcorn kernels popped. (An egg reportedly exploded, which every microwave owner can relate to.) Raytheon filed a patent for cooking food with microwaves in 1945. The first commercial “Radarange” was as tall as a person. Countertop versions arrived in the late 1960s.

**The fine print:** Spencer wasn’t the first to notice the heating effect, just the first to chase it down.

**The lesson:** When something weird happens, ask why before you wipe it up.

## 4. The pacemaker: the wrong resistor

In the late 1950s, engineer Wilson Greatbatch was building a device to *record* heart rhythms. He reached for a resistor and grabbed the wrong one. By his own account, he misread the coloured bands and fitted one that was the wrong value by a long way.

When he switched it on, it gave off a steady pulse, pause, pulse, pause: the rhythm of a beating heart. Greatbatch realised a tiny circuit like this could keep a faulty heart beating. With surgeon William Chardack he developed an implantable pacemaker, and the first patients received one in 1960.

Millions of people have had one since.

**The lesson:** “That’s not what I meant to build” can be the start of something better.

## 5. Flaked cereal: the forgotten pot of wheat

In 1894, brothers John Harvey and Will Keith Kellogg were trying to make an easy-to-digest food for patients at their health sanitarium in Michigan. They cooked some wheat and then, the story goes, left it sitting far longer than planned.

Rolled out, the softened wheat broke into thin flakes instead of a sheet. They toasted them anyway, patients loved them, and flaked cereal was born.

**The fine print:** The first flakes were wheat, not corn; Corn Flakes came a few years later. The brothers (who famously fell out) also told the story differently over the years.

**The lesson:** Sometimes the leftovers are the main event.

## 6. Vulcanised rubber: the hot stove (mostly legend)

In the 1830s, rubber went sticky in summer and cracked in winter. American inventor Charles Goodyear spent years, and most of his money, trying to fix it.

The popular story says that in 1839 he accidentally dropped a mix of rubber and sulphur onto a hot stove. It charred like leather but stayed tough and flexible: the start of vulcanisation, which made rubber useful for everything from boots to tyres.

**The fine print:** This one is more legend than fact. Goodyear did work out that heat and sulphur were the key, and he patented the process in 1844. But the stove story changes depending on who tells it, and there’s no solid record of what really happened. Thomas Hancock in England was working on the same idea too. What is well documented is the years of failed experiments it took to make the process reliable.

**The lesson:** Even “happy accidents” usually need a lot of stubborn work afterwards.

## What these stories have in common

Nobody planned these mistakes. What they shared was what came *after*: noticing something odd, getting curious instead of embarrassed, and sticking with it.
