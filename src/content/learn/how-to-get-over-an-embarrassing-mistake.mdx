You waved back at someone who was waving at the person behind you. You called your teacher “Mum”. You hit reply-all on an email that was very much not meant for all. And now, hours (or years) later, your brain still likes to replay it at 2am, in high definition, with surround sound.

Good news: you’re not broken, you’re human. Embarrassment is one of the most universal feelings there is, and it’s usually far less of a big deal than it feels. Here’s why, and what to do about it.

## Why it feels so much bigger to you

Psychologists have a name for that feeling that everyone noticed, everyone’s talking about it and everyone will remember it forever. It’s called the **spotlight effect**: we tend to overestimate how much other people notice what we do and how we look.

The classic study is gloriously daggy. In 2000, researchers at Cornell asked university students to walk into a room full of their peers wearing a T-shirt with a big picture of Barry Manilow on it. Not exactly peak cool for a uni student at the time. Afterwards, the T-shirt wearers guessed that about half the room had noticed the shirt. The real number? Roughly a quarter.

In other words, people were noticing about half as much as the wearers thought. And that was a *giant face on a shirt*.

The reason is simple. You’re the main character in your own life, so you see your stuff-up from the front row with the director’s commentary switched on. Everyone else is busy being the main character in *their* life. To them, your moment was a blip between worrying about their own email, their own haircut and what’s for dinner.

## A few other things worth remembering

- **People are more forgiving than you think.** Research on embarrassment suggests that others tend to judge us less harshly for a slip-up than we expect. Often they feel a little bit of sympathy, because they’ve been there.
- **Owning it is likeable.** Someone who laughs at their own fumble comes across as relaxed and human, not foolish.
- **The memory fades for everyone else first.** You’ll be the last person still thinking about it. Probably by a long way.

## Five steps for moving on

### 1. Name it, then shrink it

Say what happened in one plain sentence, like you’re telling a mate. “I called my boss ‘babe’ on a Zoom call.” Out loud, it often sounds less like a catastrophe and more like a story. That’s because it *is* a story.

### 2. Do the “five” check

Ask yourself: will this matter in five days? Five months? Five years? Most embarrassing moments don’t make it past the first one. The ones that do usually turn into the stories people beg you to tell at parties.

### 3. Fix what can be fixed, then stop

If there’s a genuine loose end, tie it up. Send the quick “sorry, wrong file!” email. Apologise to the person whose foot you trod on. Keep it short, and then you’re done. Over-apologising tends to make a small thing look bigger, and you’ve already got enough spotlight going on in your head.

### 4. Find the funny

Humour is one of the fastest routes from cringe to calm. Imagine telling it as an anecdote. Give it a tabloid headline. (“LOCAL WOMAN WAVES AT NOBODY, LIVES TO TELL TALE”.) You’ll notice the sting goes out of it pretty quickly once it has a punchline.

### 5. Write it down and take the lesson

This one’s our favourite, for obvious reasons. Getting it out of your head and onto the page helps you see it at its real size. Answer two questions: what happened, and what did I take from it? Even “always check who’s on the call before saying ‘love you, bye’” is a lesson. Then you can let the moment go and keep the useful bit. ## A quick note

This article is about everyday cringe: the awkward, the clumsy and the gloriously silly. If a mistake is weighing on you heavily, affecting your sleep or your mood for a long time, it’s worth chatting to someone you trust or a health professional. That’s not a failure either. It’s just good sense.
